## Design Context

### Users

Technical Home Assistant owners using a private-LAN dashboard on desktop and
mobile. They need to understand their home at a glance, move between spatial
context and exact controls quickly, and remain confident about connection,
availability, and command state.

### Brand Personality

Calm, precise, and tactile. The product should feel local, trustworthy, and
purpose-built rather than like a generic administration console.

### Aesthetic Direction

Preserve the established warm cel-shaded house, natural tinted neutrals,
restrained coral and green accents, compact translucent HUD surfaces, rounded
but not toy-like geometry, and locally bundled typography and icons. Connected
dashboards should feel like an extension of the scene rather than a separate
web app. Avoid neon-on-dark styling, generic card grids, debug-studio chrome,
remote assets, and visual effects that compete with the home model.

### Design Principles

1. Open the checked-in 3D demo home immediately with clearly labeled,
   memory-only controls and a suggested light or switch. On the first successful Home Assistant connection,
   switch permanently to real data and keep the scene navigable through every
   later connection or binding failure.
2. Make live, stale, unavailable, and pending state unambiguous without relying
   on color alone.
3. Reveal controls progressively: room first, exact device detail second.
4. Preserve strong touch targets, readable hierarchy, reduced motion, and
   intentional layouts across desktop, portrait, and landscape.
5. Prefer exact, concise English copy and restrained state feedback over
   decorative interface chrome.
6. On portrait screens, reserve the upper 43% for a centered, fully fitted
   home while setup uses the lower 57%; on short landscape screens, place setup
   on the left and resize the scene into the remaining space.

### Editor Panel Principles

1. **Give each panel one continuous surface.** Establish hierarchy through
   spacing, alignment, and typography. Remove nested cards, boxed sections, and
   decorative dividers; retain recognizable inputs and clear focus outlines.
2. **Give every object the same editing home.** Keep tools and catalog choices
   on the left, selected-object properties on the right, and Issues below the
   canvas. Use the same inspector language for architecture, furniture, devices,
   and multiple selection.
3. **Lead with the object.** Use one clear title, quieter type and location
   metadata, and a visible collapse action. Keep the header available while
   fields scroll; remove repeated headings and instructions.
4. **Compose forms as a whole.** Align labels and values, use consistent control
   heights and restrained input backgrounds, and make units secondary. Group
   related fields under Dimensions, Placement, and Appearance where applicable.
   Keep common edits visible and advanced options one disclosure away.
5. **Use color with a purpose.** Carry Cabane's warm neutrals into editor
   surfaces. Reserve accents for selection and actions, and severity colors for
   diagnostic indicators. Keep secondary text legible and state understandable
   without color alone.
6. **Keep diagnostics quiet and useful.** Start with collapsed issue counts;
   reveal readable messages, recovery guidance, and precise Locate actions on
   demand. Separate rows with spacing and subtle hover feedback. New issues
   should update counts without interrupting the current task.
7. **Protect the canvas interaction.** Preserve keyboard focus when properties
   open, selection when they collapse, and framing during gestures. Defer layout
   changes that resize the canvas until dragging, drawing, or marquee selection
   finishes.
8. **Adapt the composition to the available space.** Dock properties on wide
   screens, use a drawer on compact screens, and share one active sheet on
   mobile. Keep controls comfortably usable and frame the scene within its
   unobscured area. Retain thumbnail grids for catalog and preset choices where
   visual comparison helps.
